Why here

The company is Tasmanian.
The wool result isn't, yet.

Asparagopsis, the red seaweed behind all of this, is grown in Tasmanian waters. Sea Forest, the company that grows and processes it, is Tasmanian, and is already a named partner in this project's beef trial. That part is real, ongoing, and happening here.

Worth being precise about: the 43% methane result and the wool that ended up in a finished garment came from Congi Farm in New South Wales, using the same Sea Forest product, not from a Tasmanian sheep flock. Tasmania is where the science and the company started. It isn't yet where wool has been proven.

The active ingredient works by interrupting methane production partway through digestion, a bromoform compound naturally present in the seaweed blocks the step in a sheep's gut where methane would otherwise form. It's the same mechanism already being measured in this project's cattle trial, just not yet tested on a Tasmanian flock.

The delivery system needed to get it into a flock, lick blocks, farmer protocols, on-farm logistics, already exists here too, built and running for the beef trial. Extending it to sheep is a known, working system, not something to invent from nothing.

What this could mean

Two different reasons to pay attention to the same opportunity

For brands & mills

A single-origin, verifiable low-emissions wool supply, ready to be built on existing foundations

  • Tasmania already produces auction-topping ultrafine wool bought by the same luxury houses, and milled in the same districts, brands and mills already source from.
  • Certified sustainable wool has commanded real, tradeable premiums for years, this adds a new, currently uncontested attribute to a supply base that already meets luxury specification.
  • That pilot shows a brand doesn't need an entire industry to shift first, a single well-run property and a traceable clip is enough to build a genuine product line on.
For Tasmanian wool growers

The infrastructure already exists. What's missing is the trial.

  • SeaFeed lick blocks, farmer protocols and on-farm delivery are already running for this project's beef trial, extending to sheep isn't starting from scratch.
  • Early evidence from outside this project suggests no cost to fleece weight or wool quality, though that hasn't been tested on a Tasmanian flock yet.
  • Nothing about this would change how or where you currently sell your clip, it's a possible attribute to add to wool you already grow, not a new marketing channel you'd have to build yourself.

If you're already selling to an overseas mill, here's what would and wouldn't change

Would this affect my fleece weight or quality?

The only evidence available says no, that's from one external pilot, not a Tasmanian trial. It's a reasonable starting assumption, not a guarantee, until it's actually tested here.

Would I need to change who I sell to?

No. This is about adding a verified, traceable attribute to wool you're already producing, not replacing your existing buyer relationship. Any premium pathway would sit alongside your current sale channel, not instead of it.

What would actually be required of me, if a trial happened?

Based on how the beef trial runs today: an initial assessment of whether your operation fits the trial design, baseline data collected before any SeaFeed goes out, then ongoing weighing and fleece measurement alongside a control group. No wool trial has been scoped yet, so treat this as illustrative of the shape it would likely take, not a confirmed process.

Is there a premium on offer right now?

No. No established market premium for low-emissions wool exists yet, anywhere. That's precisely the gap a Tasmanian trial and a genuine brand relationship would need to close.
